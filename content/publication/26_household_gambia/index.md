abstract: |
  **Background.** Respiratory viruses are a major cause of morbidity in low- and middle-income
  countries (LMICs), yet little is known about their incidence, risk factors, and transmission
  dynamics in community settings. We aimed to compare the epidemiology of multiple respiratory viruses
  within households in the Gambia. **Methods.** We conducted a prospective household cohort study in a
  peri-urban region of the Gambia. Households with ≥5 members, including at least one adult and one
  child, were enrolled and followed for one year. Upper respiratory tract swabs were collected weekly,
  regardless of symptoms, and tested by RT-PCR for SARS-CoV-2, seasonal coronaviruses, human
  rhinovirus (HRV), influenza A/B, RSV A/B, parainfluenza viruses 1-4, human metapneumovirus, and
  adenovirus. We estimated incidence, symptomatic fraction, shedding duration, and household secondary
  attack rates (SARs), and explored associations with virus type and demographic variables.
  **Findings.** From 2 March 2021 to 13 June 2022, 349 participants from 52 households completed
  14,421 scheduled and 129 unscheduled visits. Respiratory viruses were detected in 11.1% of visits (n
  = 1611), representing 1423 infection episodes. HRV was most common, infecting 86.5% of participants
  (2.4 infections per person-year). Children aged <5 and 5-17 years shed virus for longer than adults
  (p = 0.0005), with longer shedding durations seen for HRV and SARS-CoV-2. Overall, 88.4% of
  infections were asymptomatic, with the highest symptomatic proportions for SARS-CoV-2 (16.3%) and
  RSV (17.7%) (p = 0.03). We found evidence for reductions in incidence risk with increasing age for
  HRV, RSV, seasonal coronaviruses, and parainfluenza viruses, with no difference for SARS-CoV-2.
  Among 719 index infections, the mean household SARs for all viruses combined was 6.7%. Infection
  with prolonged shedding (p = 0.002), specific viral species (p < 0.0001) and a lower Ct value were
  associated with increased transmission. **Interpretation.** A large burden of asymptomatic
  infections underlies the circulation of respiratory viruses within households, with key differences
  in incidence, shedding and transmission across viral species. **Funding.** This study was funded
  through a UK Research and Innovation grant.
